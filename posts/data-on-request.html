Today, a question in biology starts a project. You design the experiment, order reagents, book time on equipment, run it, find what went wrong, and run it again. The answer arrives months after the question.

I suspect that will change. Biological data may soon be something you request from an automated lab.

I come at this as a hardware and scale-up engineer. My interest is the physical side: what the equipment, the samples and the process need for that kind of lab to work.

What changes

Automated labs can already run experiments with no one at the bench. AI tools are getting better at turning a question into an experimental plan and at reading the results. Together, they make the lab a service. You state the question, and the data comes back.

That moves the value. The people who own the right questions, the data pipeline and the proof that the data is good will matter most.

What I look for

  • A closed loop. The system uses what it just learned to decide what to run next.
  • Time per answered question. This is the number that matters. Experiments per day only count if they shorten it.
  • Data you can trust. Requested data is only useful if it is reproducible. The strongest companies will show their quality checks openly.

The endpoint is easy to describe: ask a specific biological question and get the data. I’m following the companies building toward it closely, and learning from them.

Why I care: I build microfluidic systems for biological sampling. That hardware has to exist before any of this can be automated.
