In hardware, one iteration means design, fabricate, test, and find out what you got wrong. That loop takes weeks or months. Biology runs on the same clock.

Most teams accept that pace. I see it as the main opportunity.

Why speed compounds

A team that learns twice as fast ends up far more than twice as far ahead, because every lesson feeds the next design. Over a few years, a faster loop beats a better first idea.

Patents expire and designs get copied. A team that iterates ten times faster stays ahead even after it is copied, because it has already moved on.

What I look for

  • Idea to tested result: how long it takes today, and whether the team knows the number.
  • What’s shortening it: in-house fabrication, simulation before building, automated testing, AI-guided design.
  • Where the waiting happens: often the slowest step is procurement, vendors or approvals.

The goal behind all my work is to shrink the timescales of hardware and biological research.

Why I care: I have run this loop myself, from fabricating microfluidic chips to building sensing and control systems for farms.
